# Treatment Strategies

Most plans around residual disease are already making a dormancy choice.

They may not name it that way.

A useful frame is to ask which of these strategies the team is actually using.

### Keep dormant

This approach tries to hold residual cells in deep quiescence.

The logic is to reinforce signals linked to dormancy and avoid triggers that push reactivation.

This often overlaps with:

* inflammation control
* stress reduction
* preserving immune surveillance
* avoiding unnecessary microenvironment disruption

***

### Wake and kill

This approach tries to push dormant cells back into the cell cycle.

Once cells start dividing again, chemotherapy or other cytotoxic treatments may work better.

The attraction is obvious.

The risk is also obvious.

If reactivation happens without effective kill pressure, the strategy can backfire.

***

### Target cells while dormant

This approach tries to hit the survival machinery dormant cells depend on to stay alive and stay hidden.

Dormant cells are not passive.

They actively protect themselves in several ways.

* **Autophagy** — a self-recycling process that lets cells break down their own components for energy when nutrients are scarce. This may help dormant cells survive for long periods without obvious growth. [Autophagy and Cancer Dormancy](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8295230/)
* **Stress adaptation** — dormant cells can upregulate pathways such as **p38** and **NRF2** that help them tolerate oxidative stress, hypoxia, and treatment pressure that would kill more active cells. [Targeting cancer cell dormancy](https://www.nature.com/articles/s12276-020-0423-z)
* **Niche dependence** — they often anchor into a protective microenvironment, such as bone marrow or lung stroma, and rely on signals from surrounding cells to remain quiescent. [Researchers identify immune cells that keep metastatic cancer dormant](https://www.cancer.gov/news-events/cancer-currents-blog/2025/metastasis-dormant-cancer-cells-immune-system)
* **Angiogenic switching** — if the balance changes, dormant cells may trigger new blood-vessel formation, giving a small cell cluster the supply line it needs to expand. [Dormancy and angiogenesis in metastatic disease](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4668214/)

This is where repurposed drugs and targeted combinations enter the discussion.

The clearest human example so far is the **CLEVER trial**.

This is still a small early-phase study.

It is not a practice-changing standard.

It is still one of the clearest human signals that dormant-cell biology can be targeted directly.

<details>

<summary><strong>CLEVER trial summary — dormant cells assessed after three treatment cycles</strong></summary>

The **CLEVER** trial (`NCT03032406`) enrolled **51 breast-cancer survivors** with bone-marrow disseminated tumour cells.

Patients received **hydroxychloroquine**, **everolimus**, or both.

After **three treatment cycles**, more than **80%** of participants in each arm had no detectable dormant tumour cells in bone marrow.

Reported DTC reduction was:

* **HCQ alone:** **80%**
* **Everolimus alone:** **78%**
* **HCQ + everolimus:** **87%**

In practical terms, this makes **three cycles** the key early checkpoint used in the trial for assessing dormant-cell clearance.

At a median follow-up of **42 months**, recurrence-free survival remained high:

* **HCQ alone:** **91.7%**
* **Everolimus alone:** **92.9%**
* **HCQ + everolimus:** **100%**

Clearance also tracked with better outcomes.

That makes the trial important even though it remains early and small.

**Primary paper:** [Targeting dormant tumor cells to prevent recurrent breast cancer](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40897974/)

</details>

### Clear senescent survivors

Some treatments do not kill every stressed cancer cell.

Some surviving cells enter **therapy-induced senescence**.

These cells stop dividing without dying.

That can look like success at first.

The problem is that senescent cells do not simply sit there harmlessly.

They stay metabolically active.

They protect themselves in several ways.

* **SASP signalling** — senescent cells release inflammatory cytokines, growth factors, and tissue-remodelling signals. This is called the **senescence-associated secretory phenotype**, or **SASP**. It can make the surrounding environment more supportive of regrowth, invasion, and immune evasion.
* **Anti-apoptotic survival signalling** — senescent cells often stay alive by leaning on pro-survival pathways, including **BCL-2 family** signalling. That is one reason they can persist after treatment instead of dying off naturally.
* **Stress buffering** — these cells adapt to oxidative and treatment stress rather than collapsing under it. They may remain damaged, but still viable.
* **Escape potential** — some senescent tumour cells can later re-enter the cell cycle. If that happens, they may come back in a more treatment-resistant form.

This is where **senolytics** enter the discussion.

Senolytics are follow-up agents designed to eliminate senescent survivor cells.

The goal is not to replace the first treatment.

The goal is to clear what treatment left behind.

This matters because **senescence is not the same as dormancy**.

Dormant cells are often quiet and survival-adapted.

Senescent cells are arrested but inflammatory and secretory.

Some treatments may leave behind a mix of both.

That is why some residual-disease plans may need more than one layer:

* dormancy-focused thinking
* autophagy-focused thinking
* senolytic second-strike thinking

***

### BCL-2 Family Pathway Inhibitors: The Most Validated Natural Senolytics <a href="#bcl-2-family-pathway-inhibitors-the-most-validated" id="bcl-2-family-pathway-inhibitors-the-most-validated"></a>

The most extensively studied natural senolytics target the BCL-2 family of anti-apoptotic proteins — molecular shields that senescent and dormant cancer cells upregulate to evade death. Senescent cells specifically over-express BCL-2, BCL-xL, and BCL-W to maintain survival despite cellular damage. Natural compounds that inhibit these proteins dismantle the protective mechanisms, selectively pushing senescent and dormant cells toward apoptosis while sparing normal cells.

This mechanism mirrors pharmaceutical BCL-2 inhibitors like navitoclax and venetoclax, originally developed as cancer therapeutics. Combined inhibition of BCL-W and BCL-xL proves particularly effective — senescent cells rely heavily on these proteins, and dual blockade triggers selective death. Targeting all three (BCL-2, BCL-xL, BCL-W) produces superior senolytic effects compared to other combinations.

**Among natural flavonoids, fisetin consistently emerges as the most potent senolytic.** Comparative studies show fisetin has the strongest ability to reduce senescence markers and induce selective death across multiple cell types. Related flavonoids like **luteolin and quercetin** also demonstrate meaningful activity. These compounds work by upregulating pro-apoptotic proteins (Bax) while downregulating anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family members, shifting the balance toward cell death.

**Fisetin** is currently in Phase II clinical trials targeting therapy-induced senescence in breast cancer survivors post-chemotherapy. This addresses a critical need — chemotherapy creates persistent senescent cells that fuel chronic inflammation, functional decline, and potentially dormant cell reservoirs. Success would represent the first pharmacological approach specifically targeting chemotherapy-induced senescence.

BCL-2 family inhibition also impacts the tumor microenvironment. These agents eliminate senescent cancer-associated fibroblasts and suppress SASP — the inflammatory signaling that senescent cells use to remodel surroundings and reawaken dormant cells. Some BCL-2 pathway modulators additionally inhibit NF-κB, a master SASP transcription factor, creating dual senolytic and senomorphic effects.

Combining multiple BCL-2 family inhibitors overcomes heterogeneity within senescent cell populations. Since not all senescent cells express identical anti-apoptotic profiles, multi-agent strategies achieve broader clearance at lower individual doses — a principle underlying both pharmaceutical combinations and complementary natural compound approaches

### Why the strategy frame matters

A small lesion can look simple on imaging.

The biology behind it may not be simple.

For example, local treatment may be assuming one of two very different ideas:

* this focus must be eradicated now
* this focus may be lower risk if it stays controlled and biologically quiet

That is why dormancy questions help clarify the plan.

They also clarify whether the problem is really dormancy, senescence, or a mixture of both.

If recent treatment has stressed the tumour hard, the next question may not be only whether cells survived.

It may be **how** they survived.

### Useful questions to ask the oncology team

* Is the plan trying to kill, contain, or deliberately wake dormant cells?
* If chemotherapy is proposed, is the goal direct kill or re-sensitisation after reactivation?
* If local treatment is proposed, what is the whole-body strategy around residual disease elsewhere?
* Could current or recent treatment be leaving behind a **senescent survivor** population rather than only dormant cells?
* If **senolytics** are being discussed, what timing and monitoring would make sense?

### Bottom line

Dormancy science does not give one universal answer.

It gives a better set of strategy choices.

Those choices are usually **keep dormant**, **wake and kill**, **target while dormant**, or **clear therapy-induced senescent survivors**.

The key is to match the strategy to the biology the treatment is actually creating.
