# Beyond Kill It All

Dormancy biology changes how small residual disease should be interpreted.

It does **not** mean treatment should be avoided.

It means the most aggressive option is not automatically the most intelligent one.

> Experimental and clinical evidence suggests that, in some people, the immune system can hold tiny nests of cancer cells in a long‑term “stand‑off” called immune‑mediated dormancy or the equilibrium phase of cancer immuno-editing. In this state, immune cells (especially CD8 T cells and NK cells) apply just enough pressure to prevent growth but not enough to remove every last cell. Supporting this equilibrium — by protecting immune function, reducing chronic inflammation and stress, and avoiding unnecessary immunosuppression — can sometimes be as important as trying to eliminate every visible cell.\
> \
> \&#xNAN;_Cancer Dormancy: A Regulatory Role for Endogenous Immunity in Establishing and Maintaining the Tumor Dormant State._ Vaccines (Basel). 2015;3(3):597–619.\
> [https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4586469/](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4586469/)

### Why standard treatment can miss the real problem

Standard chemotherapy is best at killing _actively dividing cells_.

Dormant and slow-cycling cells often survive that pressure.

This can produce a familiar pattern:

* larger, fast-growing lesions shrink
* tiny nodules or scattered cells persist
* recurrence later comes from the cells that were hardest to hit

### Risks of a pure kill-everything approach

A kill-focused strategy can have trade-offs.

#### Selection pressure

Harsh treatment may remove the most treatment-sensitive cells first.

That can leave behind more stress-tolerant, stem-like, or dormant clones.

#### Microenvironment disruption

Local and systemic treatment can inflame or remodel the tissue niche.

In some settings, that may favour reactivation rather than long-term control.

#### Quality-of-life trade-offs

A very small stable lesion may not pose the same immediate risk as another toxic treatment round.

That question becomes more important in heavily pre-treated settings.

### How to think about tiny lesions

A small focus on imaging is not just a radiology problem.

It is a systems question.

Ask:

* does destroying this focus reduce overall risk?
* could the treatment environment wake other dormant cells?
* is stability itself an acceptable short-term goal?

### Local treatment still has a place

This is not an argument against SBRT, cryoablation, or other focal tools.

It is an argument for using them with a whole-body view of risk.

> For members considering aggressive local treatments, dormancy science does not say “never do it.” Instead, it suggests:
>
> * Think **systemically**: local destruction should ideally be paired with strategies that calm the surrounding microenvironment and systemic inflammation, and that support immune surveillance.
> * Consider whether **deep dormancy** of a very small, stable lesion might sometimes be an acceptable goal, especially if systemic options have been exhausted or are unlikely to work on slow‑cycling cells.
> * Explore whether **timing** (for example, peri-operative beta‑blockade, careful management of infection and pain, staged interventions) could reduce the risk that a big inflammatory event wakes distant dormant cells.

### Bottom line

Dormancy science pushes against reflex thinking.

Sometimes the right goal is eradication.

Sometimes the better goal is durable control with less biological disruption.
